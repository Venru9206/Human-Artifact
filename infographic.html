Mass Surveillance in American Society

Exploring the effects of the Patriot Act on technology's role in mass surveillance.

The USA Patriot Act (Patriot Act for short) or the Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism act is an act that was passed on Octover 26th, 2001, in response to the terrorist attacks of 9/11. This act was passed with the goal of aiding the federal government in preventing terrorism, but it set a precedent for acceptable interference in the private lives of American citizens that has only continued to become more invasive. With the introduction of Artifical Intelligence to the general populace, this surveillance will now reach unprecedented levels with the average American not being aware of the extent to which their privacy is being violated. Below are just some of the examples of mass surveillance that have become normalized in American society.

01. Fallen Angel Covert Recording

The CIA along with MI5 (Britain's intelligence agency) created malware called "Weeping Angel" that, once installed on Smart TVs, allowed the TV to enter a fake off state, with the screen and the power light off, while the speaker acted as a microphone covertly recording ambient noise in the room. Data would then be exfiltrated either through a hard drive or over the internet. It should be noted that this malware has to be installed physically though a hard drive, and the malware was intended specifically for 2013-era Samsung F-Series Smart TVs.

One of the things permitted by the Patriot Act is delayed notice of execution of a warrant. What this means is that potentially the CIA could search your home with a warrant and only notify you after the fact. Searching your home without your knowledge or presence gives them the freedom to install wiretaps or malware like the Fallen Angel without your knowledge. No matter how extreme or unlikely, it's the fact they have this privlege at all that should be the concern of the American people.

02. Flock Cameras

Automatic License Plate Readers (ALPRs) like that of the Flock company are AI-powered cameras that are installed on poles, traffic lights, signs and other infrastructure that capture lisence plate and vehicle data. Some things captured by flock cameras and other ALPRs are license plate number, vehicle make and model, date and time data, GPS data, and distinguishing characteristics such as dents, scratches, and stickers. The purpose of the cameras is to help locate stolen vehicles, missing persons, and track criminals. Flock claims that their cameras do not use facial recognition technology, and if that's true it's certainly the next step./p>

A hacker hacked into a single flock camera and found it took over 1.6 million images of over 50,000 vehicles in just 21 days. Some flock cameras are also now being combined with SignalTrace, a platform that connects to the bluetooth of any smart device in the car. While SignalTrace does not read the contents of the device, it does take note of unique digital signifiers. While it seems like just simple photos and signals, these data points allow anyone with access to the database to infer information about your personal routes, relationships, hobbies, employment, etc. Flock claims that their cameras do not use facial recognition technology, but each vehicle has 6 to 12 pictures taken of it, which is bound to include some of the driver. Either way, the data collection goes far beyond just that of the vehicle.

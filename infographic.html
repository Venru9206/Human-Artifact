Mass Surveillance in American Society

Exploring the Effects of Modern Technology on Mass Surveillance.

The USA Patriot Act (Patriot Act for short) or the Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism act is an act that was passed on Octover 26th, 2001, in response to the terrorist attacks of 9/11. This act was passed with the goal of aiding the federal government in preventing terrorism, but it set a precedent for acceptable interference in the private lives of American citizens that has only continued to become more invasive. With the introduction of Artifical Intelligence to the general populace, this surveillance will now reach unprecedented levels with the average American not being aware of the extent to which their privacy is being violated. Below are just some of the examples of mass surveillance that have become normalized in American society.

01. Weeping Angel Covert Recording

The CIA along with MI5 (Britain's intelligence agency) created malware called "Weeping Angel" that, once installed on Smart TVs, allowed the TV to enter a fake off state, with the screen and the power light off, while the speaker acted as a microphone covertly recording ambient noise in the room. Data would then be exfiltrated either through a hard drive or over the internet. It should be noted that this malware has to be installed physically though a hard drive, and the malware was intended specifically for 2013-era Samsung F-Series Smart TVs.

One of the things permitted by the Patriot Act is delayed notice of execution of a warrant. What this means is that potentially the CIA could search your home with a warrant and only notify you after the fact. Searching your home without your knowledge or presence gives them the freedom to install wiretaps or malware like the Weeping Angel without your knowledge. No matter how extreme or unlikely, it's the fact they have this privlege at all that should be the concern of the American people.

02. Flock Cameras

Automatic License Plate Readers (ALPRs) like that of the Flock company are AI-powered cameras that are installed on poles, traffic lights, signs and other infrastructure that capture lisence plate and vehicle data. Some things captured by flock cameras and other ALPRs are license plate number, vehicle make and model, date and time data, GPS data, and distinguishing characteristics such as dents, scratches, and stickers. The purpose of the cameras is to help locate stolen vehicles, missing persons, and track criminals. Flock claims that their cameras do not use facial recognition technology, and if that's true it's certainly the next step./p>

A hacker hacked into a single flock camera and found it took over 1.6 million images of over 50,000 vehicles in just 21 days. Some flock cameras are also now being combined with SignalTrace, a platform that connects to the bluetooth of any smart device in the car. While SignalTrace does not read the contents of the device, it does take note of unique digital signifiers. While it seems like just simple photos and signals, these data points allow anyone with access to the database to infer information about your personal routes, relationships, hobbies, employment, etc. Flock claims that their cameras do not use facial recognition technology, but each vehicle has 6 to 12 pictures taken of it, which is bound to include some of the driver. Either way, the data collection goes far beyond just that of the vehicle.

03. Keystroke and Gait Identification

We all know our phone tracks our movement--the step counter--but it tracks more than strictly the steps you take. The internal gyroscope tracks your sitting, standing, stair climbing, and walking movements as well as features such as tilt and stride length. AI can use your gait patterns to identify you with up to 98% accuracy in a controlled setting, though this does decrease in the real world. Gait can also be used to identify a person from up to 165 feet away, even with the back turned or the face covered.

Keystrokes are another example of biometric data that most people don't realize is being collected. A person can be identified based on the duration of each tap, pressure applied, finger contact size, and typing rhythm. This works for both a physical keyboard, and a phone screen. It's possible that typing biometrics may be used in the future as a form of two factor authentication. It's fine if you elect to do so, but as of now did you ever consent to having your typing habits collected?

04. Wifi Tracking

Wifi signals can pass through walls but will refract off of the human body. These refractions can be mapped to determine the shape, size, and position of the person it has refracted off of. AI can map the signal changes to particular points on the human body, reconstructing positions in 3D. You do not need a device on you to be tracked this way, the wifi from your home router is enough. There is no special technology involved in this process; signals bounce off of objects, and the refractions can be used to collect details about the object it has bounced off of, no different than echolocation in bats, though it is more precise. It makes wifi a natural surveillance tool. Wifi is pretty much in all public and residential spaces, and with AI can identify someone with near perfect accuracy. Because it's almost impossible to avoid and requires no special tools to track, wifi has the potential to be one of the most powerful surveillance tools we have.

05. Surveillance Pricing

Surveillance pricing is a form of algorithmic pricing that uses personal customer data to set a unique price pertaining to the individual for an item. This si common among retailers such as Amazon as well as airlines. Algorithmic pricing takes into account a users income, shopping habits, demographics, and percieved emotional state for setting a price. The goal of algorithmic pricing is to set the highest price the customer is willing to pay to extract as much profit from each individual as possible. Companies and retailers also use data such as mouse movements, items left in shopping cart, and product searches for their algorithmic pricing. They may also buy personal data from a data brocker in order to make more accurate asumptions about the limits of consumers' spending thresholds. Walmart is implementing digital price tags in all of its stores by 2027, and though they promise they won't use them for dynamic pricing, companies have never been very good at keeping promises.
